# EDUCATION.md — Solar Storm

> The educational component (the **E** in GAMES). The game's threats map to real solar physics; this is
> what a player should walk away understanding.

## The Sun is a magnetic, not a calm, star
The Sun is a ball of plasma — ionized gas whose churning, electrically charged motion generates intense,
tangled **magnetic fields**. Those fields, not fire, drive everything called "space weather." The game's
roiling, granulated surface stands in for **convection**: hot plasma rises, cools, and sinks in cells
(real granules are ~1,000 km across, each lasting minutes).

## Sunspots — where the magnetism knots up
**Sunspots** are regions where bundles of magnetic field break through the surface. The field suppresses
local convection, so the gas is **cooler (~3,500 K vs ~5,500 K) and therefore looks dark** against the
brighter surroundings. They are not holes or cold spots in any absolute sense — only relatively cooler.
Sunspots are the storehouses of magnetic energy. In the game a sunspot's **building, brightening rim** is
that energy accumulating; **quelling it early** models active regions that quietly decay instead of erupting.

## Solar flares — the fast flash
When stressed magnetic field lines suddenly snap and **reconnect**, they release stored energy as a
**solar flare**: a brilliant burst of light and X-rays/UV radiation. Flares travel at the speed of light,
so their radiation reaches Earth in about **8 minutes** — there's almost no warning. That's why the game's
flares are *small and fast* and score the most to deflect: in reality they ionize the upper atmosphere and
can black out radio and GPS within minutes.

## Coronal mass ejections — the slow, heavy punch
A **coronal mass ejection (CME)** is a billion-ton cloud of magnetized plasma blasted off the Sun. Unlike
a flare, a CME is *matter* and travels far slower — typically **1–3 days** to cross to Earth — which is why
the game's CMEs are big, slow clouds (and why a missed sunspot *erupts into one*). When a CME's magnetic
field hits Earth's magnetosphere it drives a **geomagnetic storm**.

## The ~11-year solar cycle
Solar activity waxes and wanes on an average **~11-year cycle**, swinging from **solar minimum** (few
sunspots, calm) to **solar maximum** (many sunspots, frequent flares and CMEs) and back. The Sun's magnetic
field actually flips polarity each cycle (a full magnetic cycle is ~22 years). The game compresses one cycle
into 60 seconds — the climbing meter ramps minimum → maximum, exactly mirroring how threats multiply as the
real Sun grows active.

## Why we care — satellites and the power grid
Space weather is a genuine infrastructure hazard, which is what the player is defending:
- **Satellites:** flares and energetic particles degrade solar panels and electronics, scramble GPS, and
  can knock spacecraft offline; CME-driven drag can drop satellites from orbit.
- **Power grids:** a strong CME induces **geomagnetically induced currents** in long transmission lines that
  can overload and destroy transformers. The **1989 Québec** storm blacked out millions for 9 hours; the
  **1859 Carrington Event** set telegraph offices on fire and would be catastrophic for a modern grid.
- **Auroras** are the pretty side effect — the same particles funneling down field lines to glow over the poles.

## Takeaways (the testable beats)
1. The Sun's activity is driven by **magnetic fields**, and **sunspots** are where that magnetism concentrates.
2. **Flares** = fast light/radiation (~8 min); **CMEs** = slow plasma clouds (~1–3 days). Different threats,
   different timing — exactly the game's fast-flare / slow-CME split.
3. Activity follows the **~11-year solar cycle** (minimum ↔ maximum).
4. Space weather threatens **satellites and power grids** — a real reason to forecast and "raise shields."
