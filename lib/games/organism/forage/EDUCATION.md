# EDUCATION.md — Forage

> The E in GAMES. What a player actually learns, why the mechanic teaches it, and
> the real biology behind it.

## The lesson: an animal lives on an energy budget

Every animal runs a ledger it can never overdraw for long:

    net energy = energy in (food) − energy out (everything you do to get it and stay alive)

"Energy out" is not just exercise. It is **basal metabolism** (the cost of simply being alive — keeping
warm, pumping blood, repairing tissue) **plus** the cost of **foraging itself** — moving, searching,
chasing, fleeing. Food only counts as a gain *after* you subtract what it cost to obtain. A meal that
takes more energy to reach than it delivers is a net **loss**, even though you ate. Run the ledger
negative long enough and the animal starves. This game is that ledger, made playable.

## Why the mechanic teaches it (not just decorates it)

- **The energy meter is the ledger.** It rises when you eat and falls when you act. You watch the
  budget move in real time — there is no hidden bookkeeping, just intake minus expenditure.
- **Movement costs energy, and the cost is charged against each meal.** When you eat, the game checks:
  did this food give more than the movement energy you spent reaching it? If yes → **EFFICIENT**
  (streak + bonus). If no → the chase was a net loss (streak resets). This is **optimal foraging
  theory** turned into a scoreboard: foragers are selected to maximize energy *gained per energy
  spent*, not gross calories eaten.
- **Resting is a real strategy.** Standing still spends only the basal drain, so waiting for nearby food
  lets the next bite come in cheap and efficient. Real animals do exactly this — they rest, conserve,
  and forage in bursts rather than chasing every distant scrap.
- **Predators are an energy cost, not just a death threat.** Being near a predator drains energy (the
  metabolic cost of fear and flight — racing heart, sprint muscles), and a bite costs a big chunk. Time
  spent avoiding predators is time and energy *not* spent feeding. Foraging is always a trade-off
  between getting food and not becoming food.
- **The budget tightens over the round.** Food gets scarcer, the basal cold/hunger drain rises, and
  predators multiply — modeling a harsh season (winter, drought) when expenditure climbs and intake
  falls, exactly when energy management matters most.

## The real biology

- **Basal metabolic rate (BMR)** is the energy an animal burns at rest just to stay alive. For many
  animals it is a *large* share of the daily budget — you are spending energy every second, asleep or
  awake. That is the game's constant background drain.
- **Optimal foraging theory** predicts animals forage to maximize net energy intake rate. A classic
  result: a predator should ignore a far-off, low-value prey if a closer or richer option exists,
  because travel and handling time are energy spent for nothing. The "EFFICIENT vs inefficient" grade is
  this rule.
- **Thermoregulation** (staying warm in the cold) is metabolically expensive for endotherms ("warm-
  blooded" animals). Cold weather *raises* energy expenditure — the harsher late-game drain and the
  cold vignette are that effect.
- **Energy reserves buffer the budget.** Animals bank energy as fat (and, for our potato forager, as
  starch) so a few bad foraging hours don't kill them. The meter you fill is that reserve; hitting zero
  is starvation. Recovery after a collapse models scraping back from an energy crisis.
- **Foraging is a trade-off with predation risk.** "Forage more = eat more = more exposure to
  predators" is one of the central tensions in behavioral ecology. The fear-ring drain makes it literal.

## What a player should be able to say afterward

- "Food is only a gain after you subtract what it cost to get — net energy, not gross."
- "Chasing far-off food can cost more energy than it's worth, so it's smart to eat nearby and rest."
- "Just staying alive (and warm) burns energy constantly — that's basal metabolism."
- "Predators cost energy even when they don't catch you, because fleeing and fear are expensive."

## Stretch / discussion

- Why might a starving animal take *more* predation risk than a well-fed one?
- If moving costs energy, why don't animals just sit still? (Basal drain still runs — you must eat.)
- How does cold weather change the foraging math, and why do some animals hibernate instead?
- A potato sprouting in the dark burns its own stored starch with no food coming in — how long can it
  run a negative budget before it must reach light? (Its own forage clock.)
