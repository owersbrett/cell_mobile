# EDUCATION.md — Bond Lab

> The educational contract (the **E** in GAMES). The lesson is not a screen the player skips — it
> is the win condition. To score, you must apply real chemistry; the mechanic *is* the teaching.

## Core concept: chemical bonding types

Atoms join in three ways, decided by the **character** of the two atoms involved:

| Bond | Forms between | What the electrons do | Examples in game |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ionic** | metal + nonmetal | one atom **transfers** an electron to the other; the resulting `+`/`−` ions attract | NaCl, CaO, MgO, AlCl₃ |
| **Covalent** | nonmetal + nonmetal | the atoms **share** a pair of electrons between their nuclei | O₂, H₂, H₂O, CO₂, CH₄ |
| **Metallic** | metal + metal | atoms pool a **delocalized sea** of electrons that flows around fixed cores | Na, Cu, Fe lattices; Cu·Fe, Fe·Al alloys |

The game makes this visible: on a correct call you *watch* the electron leap across (ionic), orbit
the midpoint (covalent), or swarm both atoms (metallic). The difference is shown, not told.

## The deeper signal: electronegativity (EN)

Each atom is labeled with its Pauling **electronegativity** — its pull on shared electrons.

- **Low EN (< ~2.0)** = metallic character; the atom gives electrons up easily.
- **High EN (> ~2.0)** = nonmetallic character; the atom grips electrons tightly.

EN is the clue that lets a player reason about an unfamiliar pair, and it sets up the game's central
misconception-buster: **a large EN gap does not always mean ionic.**

## The lesson that accelerates: polar covalent

Late-game tier-1 pairs are the trap. Two **nonmetals** with a wide EN gap — HF (1.78), H₂O (1.24),
HCl (0.96), CO₂ (0.89) — pull electrons *unevenly*, making the bond **polar**, which tempts a wrong
"ionic" answer. But two nonmetals **share** (they never fully transfer), so the bond is **covalent**.
The game flags these `· polar covalent` on a correct call — so the player learns the exact boundary
between "polar covalent" and "ionic" by being baited at it and corrected.

Parallel trap on the metallic side: **alloys** (Cu·Fe, Fe·Al, Mg·Al) — two *different* metals still
form a metallic bond, not an ionic one. Different EN, same answer.

## Misconceptions this game targets

1. *"Big electronegativity difference = ionic."* → False for two nonmetals; it is polar covalent.
2. *"Sharing vs transferring is arbitrary."* → It is decided by metal/nonmetal character.
3. *"A metallic bond needs identical atoms."* → Alloys bond two different metals metallically.
4. *"Covalent bonds are always nonpolar."* → Polarity is a spectrum set by the EN gap.

## How it maps to potato biology

- **Covalent:** H₂O and CO₂ — the covalent feedstock photosynthesis bonds into glucose in the leaf.
- **Ionic:** K⁺, Ca²⁺, and chloride salts the tuber draws from soil to hold turgor and build walls.
- **Metallic:** the iron and copper centers in the potato's respiratory enzymes — metallic-bonded
  metals doing redox work.

Bonding type is the first rung of the cell ladder: pick the right bond and you have built the
molecule that builds the organelle that builds the cell.
