# EDUCATION — Phase Change

**Scale:** molecular · **Concept:** states of matter, phase transitions, and
**latent heat**.

## What the player learns by playing
The lesson is delivered through the mechanic, not a quiz:

1. **Three states of matter.** Molecules are drawn as a grid that visibly
   changes behaviour with energy:
   - **Solid** — molecules lock into a rigid **lattice** (bonds drawn between
     neighbours); they only vibrate in place.
   - **Liquid** — bonds break and molecules **flow** past each other while
     staying close.
   - **Gas** — molecules have enough energy to **fly apart** and fill the
     container.

2. **Temperature ≠ energy.** The bottom strip plots **temperature vs. energy
   added**. Adding energy usually raises temperature — but at the melting and
   boiling points the curve goes **flat**.

3. **Latent heat (the key idea).** On those flat plateaus, energy you pour in
   goes into **breaking the bonds that hold the state together** (latent heat of
   fusion at melting, latent heat of vaporization at boiling) rather than
   raising the temperature. The player *feels* this: they keep heating but the
   thermometer stalls, and the matter is stuck "in transition" — neither state —
   until enough energy has been delivered to finish the phase change.

4. **Every substance is different.** Each material (water, wax, mercury, glass,
   iron) has its own melting and boiling points and its own plateau widths.
   Mercury is liquid across a wide range; iron needs enormous energy just to
   melt. Switching substances each round shows that phase behaviour is a
   property of the material.

## Why it sticks
The player must *manage* the plateau to win — overshoot and they fly past the
target state; stall too long and ambient cooling drags them back. The frustration
of "I'm adding heat and nothing's happening" is exactly the intuition for latent
heat that a textbook diagram can't give.

## Real-world hooks
- Ice water stays at 0 °C until all the ice has melted (latent heat of fusion).
- A pot of boiling water stays at 100 °C no matter how high the burner.
- Sweating cools you because evaporation absorbs latent heat from your skin.
