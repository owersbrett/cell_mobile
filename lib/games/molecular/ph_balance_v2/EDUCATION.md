# EDUCATION — pH Balance v2

**Scale:** molecular · **Concept:** acids vs bases · the 0–14 pH scale ·
indicators · neutralization · the titration curve and its steep equivalence
point · CO₂ → carbonic acid.

> The learning arc: atoms → molecules → those molecules ARE the potato. pH
> Balance teaches the rule that governs how molecules behave in water:
> **acidity, the pH scale, and neutralization.** v2 keeps every bit of that and
> *shows* the steep curve instead of only making you feel it.

## What the player learns by playing
The teaching is the controls, not a popup.

1. **The 0–14 pH scale is a real object.** The right-edge strip is a
   universal-indicator ramp: red (strong acid, 0) → orange → yellow → **green
   (neutral, 7)** → blue → indigo → **violet (strong base, 14)**. The beaker
   liquid is the same color as its current pH. Play one round and you can read
   pH off a color for life.

2. **Acids lower pH, bases raise it — and they cancel.** The ACID button is
   literally **H⁺ (pH ▼)** and BASE is **OH⁻ (pH ▲)**. Adding acid to a basic
   liquid walks it down toward 7; adding base to an acidic one walks it up. You
   *feel* neutralization: opposing reagents driving toward the middle.

3. **The titration curve is steep near neutral — now SHOWN, not just felt.**
   Each drop's effect is multiplied by a gaussian peaked at pH 7, so near
   neutral one drop **leaps** across the equivalence point. v1 made you feel the
   overshoot but never showed *why*. v2 draws **predictive ghost ticks** on the
   scale — the spot where your next ACID/BASE drop would land — and shades the
   **STEEP zone** around pH 7. When you hover near neutral, the ghost ticks fan
   far away from the needle: you can *see* that a single drop here is a huge
   swing. That is the near-vertical jump of a real acid–base titration curve,
   made visible. Far from 7 the ghosts hug the needle — the liquid is buffered.

4. **Titrating near the equivalence point means going dropwise.** Because near-7
   taps are too coarse to sit inside a tight band, you learn to **pump**: add a
   drop, let the drift carry the needle through the band, add another. That is
   exactly how a chemist adds titrant dropwise approaching the endpoint.

5. **CO₂ acidifies water (carbonic acid) — and it never stops.** The liquid
   self-acidifies every second (in v2 from the very first level), the same
   chemistry by which dissolved CO₂ forms carbonic acid and lowers the pH of
   rain, oceans, and soil. To hold a basic target you must keep adding base:
   homeostasis as a verb.

## Why v2 sticks better
The frustration of v1 — "I tapped and the needle moved late, then overshot" —
read as input lag. v2 lands the tap instantly and shows where the next one goes,
so overshoot becomes a *decision* ("don't tap, I'll fly past 7") instead of a
surprise. And the hold is no longer dead time: the constant CO₂ creep means
every locked target is sustained micro-control, which is the felt experience of
*holding a chemical equilibrium*.

## Carry-out idea
Near the equivalence point of a titration the pH swings wildly for a tiny amount
of added acid or base — that is why a chemist adds the last drops one at a time
and why a single drop can flip an indicator from clear to pink. You just lived
that steepness with your thumb.

## Potato angle
Potatoes prefer slightly acidic soil (~pH 5.0–6.0). Growers **titrate their
fields**: lime (a base) to raise pH, elemental sulfur to lower it. Common scab is
suppressed in acidic soil and worsens when fields are over-limed toward neutral —
so a grower deliberately *holds the band* below 7, which is the game's near-
neutral danger zone reframed. The beaker is the field; balancing pH is balancing
the potato's chemistry, drop by drop.
