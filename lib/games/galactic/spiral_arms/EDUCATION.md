# EDUCATION.md — Spiral Arms

> The **E** in GAMES. The lesson is not bolted on — it *is* the mechanic. Playing the game is rehearsing
> the correct mental model.

## The "that's not how I pictured it" lesson
Almost everyone pictures a spiral galaxy as a **pinwheel**: a fixed set of stars locked into arm shapes,
the whole thing spinning around like a frisbee with the arms painted on. **That picture is wrong**, and the
game is built to break it.

## Why the pinwheel picture can't be true: the winding problem
Galaxies do **not** rotate as a rigid body. They rotate **differentially** — a star near the core completes
an orbit in a fraction of the time a star at the rim does (in the Milky Way, the Sun takes ~230 million
years for one lap; inner stars are much faster). 

If the arms were fixed lines of stars, differential rotation would **wind them up** tighter and tighter
every rotation. Within just a handful of galactic rotations the arms would smear into a featureless,
uniform disk. This is the famous **winding problem** (or *winding dilemma*). Yet we see crisp spiral arms in
galaxies that are billions of years — and hundreds of rotations — old. The pinwheel model is ruled out.

## The resolution: spiral arms are DENSITY WAVES
The arms are not a *thing* made of the same stars — they are a **pattern**, a region of higher density that
the stars stream **through**. The standard analogy is a **traffic jam**:

- On a highway, a slow-moving jam can sit in one spot (or crawl slowly) for hours.
- Individual cars drive *into* the back of the jam, bunch up, crawl through, and accelerate out the front.
- The jam — the *pattern of high density* — persists, even though no single car stays in it.

A spiral arm is the same. Stars (and gas) orbit the galaxy fast; as they enter the arm they **bunch up and
slow**, then continue on out the other side. The **density wave** itself rotates slowly and rigidly (one
"pattern speed"), so it does **not** wind up — it sidesteps the winding problem entirely. This is the
**Lin–Shu density wave theory** (C.C. Lin & Frank Shu, 1964).

## Why the arms look bright (and blue)
The compression in the wave squeezes gas clouds and triggers **bursts of star formation**. The most massive
new stars are very hot, blue, and **short-lived** — they burn out before they can leave the arm. So the arm
is permanently outlined by bright blue young stars and glowing star-forming regions, even though the
individual stars are constantly being replaced. The arm is lit up not because old stars are stuck there,
but because it's where stars are *born*.

## How the game teaches it (mechanic ↔ concept map)
| In the game | The real physics |
|---|---|
| Inner stars visibly outrun outer stars | Differential rotation |
| Arms shear and smear if you do nothing | The winding problem |
| Coherence meter bleeding away | Why a *fixed* pattern can't survive |
| The slow-rotating arm spines | The density wave's slow pattern speed |
| Pulsing on the beat to keep arms crisp | The density wave continually reasserting the pattern |
| Stars brightening as they cross the spine | Compression → bursts of (blue) star formation |
| Stars sliding *through* the bright arms | Stars stream through the wave; they don't live in it |

## One-sentence takeaway
**A spiral arm is a traffic jam of stars, not a fixed pinwheel of stars — that's how it survives a galaxy
that's tearing itself apart by spinning.**
