# EDUCATION.md — Galaxy Merger

> The educational component (the **E** in GAMES). What a player should walk away understanding, why
> the mechanic *is* the lesson, and the fact flares that surface during play.

## The big idea
**Galaxies collide and merge — and you are literally playing the physics astronomers use to model it.**
When two galaxies pass close, their gravity reshapes them: streamers of stars called **tidal tails**
are flung out, the cores spiral together, and two spiral galaxies eventually fuse into a single,
rounder **elliptical** galaxy. The astonishing part: the stars themselves almost never hit each
other. Galaxies are mostly empty space, so they pass *through* one another — it's **gravity**, not
collisions, that does the reshaping.

## Why the mechanic teaches it
The game is a **restricted three-body simulation** — the same approach Alar & Juri Toomre used in
their landmark 1972 study that first showed tidal tails are a natural result of close galaxy passes:

- A **heavy host core** (fixed) and a **moving intruder core**.
- A disk of **massless "stars"** that feel the gravity of **both** cores.

You don't sculpt the tails by hand — you set the **approach** (impact parameter + speed), and the
tails *emerge* from the math, exactly as they do in real simulations and in real galaxies. Reading
the **MERGE / FLYBY** preview is the player internalizing how impact parameter and speed decide
whether two galaxies capture and merge or just slingshot past.

## Three things a player learns
1. **Tidal forces stretch galaxies into tails.** Gravity pulls harder on the near side of a galaxy
   than the far side; that *difference* (a tidal force — the same physics that raises Earth's ocean
   tides) draws a **bridge** toward the companion and flings a long **tail** out the far side.
2. **Galaxies pass through each other — stars rarely collide.** The space between stars is enormous.
   In a merger the disks interpenetrate; what changes their shape is the mutual gravity, not impacts.
3. **Spirals merge into ellipticals.** A clean merger scrambles the ordered, rotating disks into a
   single pressure-supported blob — an elliptical galaxy. Many big ellipticals are merger remnants.

## The headline example
The **Milky Way and Andromeda (M31)** are approaching at ~110 km/s and are expected to begin merging
in roughly **4–5 billion years**, eventually forming a single elliptical galaxy sometimes nicknamed
"Milkomeda." Earth's Sun will most likely survive — flung to a new orbit, but not destroyed —
because, again, individual stars almost never collide.

## Fact flares (fire during play, in `_mergeFacts` / `_flybyFacts`)
- "Clean merge — two spirals settle into one elliptical galaxy."
- "The cores fall together, but the stars mostly pass THROUGH each other."
- "Andromeda is on this exact course toward the Milky Way."
- "Tidal forces drew those streaming tails — the same force as ocean tides."
- "Too fast — the galaxies slingshot apart, stars flung into the void." (flyby)
- "Stars rarely collide — but a bad pass still scatters the disks." (flyby)

## Real merger touchstones (for flavor / future fact cards)
- **The Antennae Galaxies (NGC 4038/4039)** — the textbook nearby merger, with two enormous tidal
  tails (the "antennae" that give it its name).
- **The Mice (NGC 4676)** — a pair mid-pass, each trailing a long, thin tidal tail.
- **The Tadpole Galaxy** — a single disrupted galaxy dragging a 280,000-light-year tail.

## Potato angle (light — optional, don't force)
There is more dark matter wrapped around one merging galaxy than there are atoms in every potato
ever grown. Keep it as an end-of-round aside, never a teaching beat.
