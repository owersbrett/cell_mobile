# EDUCATION.md — Black-Hole Heart

> The E in GAMES. What a player actually learns, why the mechanic teaches it, and
> the real science behind it.

## The lesson: the galaxy turns around a supermassive black hole

At the center of the Milky Way sits **Sagittarius A\*** (Sgr A\*), a
**supermassive black hole** of about **4.3 million solar masses** packed into a
region smaller than Mercury's orbit. We know its mass because we have watched
stars (the "S-stars", especially **S2**) sweep around it in tight, fast,
decades-long orbits — orbits that only such a tiny, monstrous mass can produce.
In 2022 the Event Horizon Telescope released the first image of the glowing ring
around its shadow.

This game puts you in charge of that heart. The single overwhelming mass at the
center is the black hole; the lethal black disk is its **event horizon**; the
glowing rings are its **accretion disk**. Your stars are the S-stars, and the
whole skill is keeping them in orbit on the edge of the abyss.

## Why the mechanic teaches it (not just decorates it)

- **The event horizon is a one-way door.** Cross `r ≤ horizon` and the star is
  gone — "swallowed", fed to the black hole, streak reset. The horizon is the
  radius of no return: not a surface, but the boundary inside which not even light
  can escape. The game makes it the literal fail line.
- **Close orbits are FAST.** Orbital speed is `v = √(GM/r)`: halve the radius and
  the required speed rises by √2. The preview shows it — a tight orbit needs a
  much harder, more precise fling than a wide one. Hug the horizon and your star
  visibly whips around.
- **Close orbits are PRECARIOUS.** Near a black hole gravity grows *faster* than
  Newton predicts (the game's `(1 + relK·horizon/r)` term stands in for General
  Relativity), so close orbits **precess** — the ellipse itself rotates, exactly
  as S2's orbit precesses around Sgr A\* (measured by the GRAVITY instrument,
  confirming Einstein). Combined with faster decay inside the **ISCO** (innermost
  stable circular orbit), the most rewarding orbits are the ones most likely to
  fall in.
- **Accretion is real.** Matter spiraling toward a black hole doesn't fall
  straight in; it forms a hot, glowing **accretion disk** and slowly loses energy
  inward. The game's orbit decay *is* accretion drag, and the disk you see is the
  gas heated to brilliance on its way down. The photon ring flares each time the
  hole is fed.

## The real science

- **Why so close = so fast.** Bound orbital speed scales as `v ∝ 1/√r`. The
  S-stars near Sgr A\* reach a few **thousand kilometers per second** at closest
  approach — a sizable fraction of a percent of light speed — precisely because
  they orbit so close to such a huge mass.
- **The event horizon.** Its size is the Schwarzschild radius, `r_s = 2GM/c²`. For
  Sgr A\* that's roughly **12 million km** (about 17 solar radii) — large for a
  star, vanishingly small for something 4 million times the Sun's mass. Inside it,
  escape would require traveling faster than light, which nothing can.
- **ISCO.** General Relativity says there is an **innermost stable circular
  orbit** at `3 r_s` (for a non-spinning hole). Inside it, no stable circular orbit
  exists — matter inevitably spirals in. That's why the game's inner ring decays
  orbits so fast.
- **Accretion power.** A black hole shines not from itself but from its disk:
  infalling gas converts up to ~10% of its rest mass to light (far more efficient
  than fusion's ~0.7%), which is why feeding supermassive black holes power the
  brightest objects in the universe, **quasars**.
- **It anchors the galaxy.** Sgr A\* doesn't "hold the galaxy together" by gravity
  alone (the galaxy's mass dwarfs it), but every supermassive black hole is woven
  into its host through the **M–σ relation** — black-hole mass tracks the galaxy's
  central star speeds, hinting they grew up together.

## What a player should be able to say afterward

- "Our galaxy has a supermassive black hole at its center — Sgr A\*, about 4
  million Suns."
- "The event horizon is the point of no return; cross it and nothing comes back."
- "Closer orbits are faster — `v = √(GM/r)` — and near a black hole they precess
  and are unstable, which is why the close, high-scoring orbits keep falling in."
- "The glow is an accretion disk: gas heated white-hot as it slowly spirals in."

## Stretch / discussion

- If the horizon is a point of no return, how do we 'see' the black hole at all?
  (Answer: by its disk and by the stars orbiting it.)
- Why is there a *smallest* stable orbit (the ISCO) but not a smallest planet
  orbit around the Sun?
- The image of Sgr A\* shows a bright ring around a dark center — what is the dark
  center, and why is the ring there?
