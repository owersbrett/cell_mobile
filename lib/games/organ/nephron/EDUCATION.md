# EDUCATION.md — Nephron

> The **E** in GAMES. The lesson is not bolted on — it *is* the mechanic. Playing the game is rehearsing
> the correct mental model of how a kidney cleans blood.

## The "that's not how I pictured it" lesson
Most people picture the kidney as a **sieve**: blood goes in, a filter catches the bad stuff and lets the
good stuff through, done. **That picture is backwards.** The kidney's first move is to dump *almost
everything* small out of the blood — good and bad alike — and then **actively claw back the good stuff**.
Cleaning the blood is not "catching the waste"; it's "rescuing the nutrients and letting the rest go."

## The nephron: the kidney's working unit
Each kidney holds about **a million nephrons**. A nephron is a tiny tube with a filter at its head. The
game is one nephron, sped up and made visible.

### Step 1 — Filtration (the glomerulus)
Blood enters a tuft of leaky capillaries called the **glomerulus**. Blood *pressure* forces water and small
molecules out of the blood and into the tubule. This filter is **not selective** — it sorts by size, not by
worth. Water, glucose, amino acids, ions, urea, toxins: if it's small enough, it gets pushed into the
filtrate. Big things (blood cells, proteins) stay behind. Roughly **180 litres** of filtrate are produced
per day — far more than the body could afford to lose.

### Step 2 — Selective reabsorption (the tubule)
As the filtrate flows down the tubule, cells lining the wall **actively pump the valuable molecules back**
into the surrounding blood vessels (the peritubular capillaries):
- **~100% of glucose and amino acids** are reabsorbed (you should have *no* glucose in healthy urine).
- **~99% of the water and most ions** are reabsorbed — the body keeps what it needs and **discards the
  surplus** (this is how the kidney regulates blood salt and volume).
- **Waste is left behind**: urea, creatinine, uric acid, ammonia, drug metabolites. Not reabsorbed → it
  keeps flowing → it becomes urine.

So the urine is essentially **the leftovers** — what the tubule chose *not* to rescue.

## Why "needed sodium" vs "excess sodium" is the hardest call
The kidney doesn't reabsorb *all* sodium or *none* — it reabsorbs **the amount the body needs** and lets
the **excess** pass into urine. That's how it keeps blood pressure and salt balance steady. The game's
LV3 twist — identical-looking `Sodium` and `Sodium · EXCESS` molecules — is exactly this judgment call:
the same ion, kept or discarded depending on the body's current need.

## How the game teaches it (mechanic ↔ concept map)
| In the game | The real physiology |
|---|---|
| Molecules pour into the tubule from the glomerulus | Filtration — pressure pushes everything small out of blood |
| Default: a molecule falls out the bottom into URINE | Anything not reabsorbed becomes urine |
| Flicking good molecules LEFT into the blood | Selective reabsorption (the active step) |
| Reabsorbing earns points; missing a nutrient hurts | The body can't afford to lose glucose / water |
| Keeping waste in blood drains BLOOD PURITY hard | Failure to excrete = toxins build up in the blood (uremia) |
| `Sodium` vs `Sodium · EXCESS` | The kidney reabsorbs *needed* ions, discards the surplus |
| Blood purity hitting zero ends the run | Kidney failure — the blood is no longer being cleaned |

## One-sentence takeaway
**A kidney doesn't strain the bad stuff out of your blood — it dumps almost everything out, then actively
reabsorbs the good stuff back; whatever it doesn't rescue is urine.**

## Sources / further reading (for fact-checking copy)
- Glomerular filtration & selective reabsorption — standard human-physiology (e.g. GCSE/A-level Biology,
  Guyton & Hall *Textbook of Medical Physiology*).
- ~1 million nephrons per kidney; ~180 L/day filtered, ~1–2 L excreted as urine.
- Glucose & amino acids ~fully reabsorbed in the proximal tubule; sodium/water reabsorption is regulated
  (aldosterone / ADH) to keep the *needed* amount and discard the excess.
