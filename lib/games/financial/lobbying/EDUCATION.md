# EDUCATION.md — Lobbying

> What this game teaches: the core ideas of **public choice economics** — how organized interests buy
> influence, why **concentrated benefits beat diffuse costs**, and why a dollar of influence pays off
> most at the margin. The framing is **satire**: you play the spud lobby, the bills are ridiculous, and
> the officials are potatoes. The mechanics underneath are the real lesson; the jokes are the spoonful
> of butter that makes it go down.

- **Scale (cell):** financial
- **Game on this scale:** `lobbying` (this game) — joins "Market Trader" on the financial scale.
- **Disclaimer:** this is a **caricature** of how influence works, exaggerated for play. Real lobbying
  is legal, regulated, and mostly mundane (information, testimony, relationships); the cartoon
  bag-of-cash version here is for teaching the *incentive structure*, not a how-to.

---

## Part 1 — Public choice in one sentence

**Public choice** applies the tools of economics to politics: it assumes voters, politicians, and
interest groups each pursue their **own incentives**, the same way buyers and sellers do in a market.
Government outcomes are then the *equilibrium* of those incentives — not the pure expression of "the
public good." The game is a tiny public-choice machine: you, the lobby, optimize; the officials
respond to spending; bills pass or fail as the incentives net out.

## Part 2 — Concentrated benefits, diffuse costs (the big one)

Every bill in the game carries a "**who-wins · who-pays**" subtitle, e.g. *"Tax credit for Big Fry ·
paid by every grocery shopper."* That is the single most important idea in the whole game:

- A **concentrated benefit** goes to a small, organized group (the fry industry gets a fat subsidy).
  Each member gains a lot, so they'll spend real money and effort to get it.
- The matching **diffuse cost** is spread thinly over millions of people (a fraction of a cent per
  grocery trip). No individual loses enough to bother fighting it.

Result: the organized few **out-lobby** the unorganized many, even when the total cost to society
exceeds the total benefit. This asymmetry — first sharpened by **Mancur Olson** in *The Logic of
Collective Action* (1965) — explains a huge amount of real-world policy: tariffs, subsidies,
occupational licensing, tax carve-outs. The game lets you *be* the concentrated interest and feel why
the strategy works.

## Part 3 — Rent-seeking

When a group spends resources to **capture a benefit from government** rather than to create new value,
economists call it **rent-seeking** (Gordon Tullock, Anne Krueger). Your entire war chest is
rent-seeking spend: it produces no fries, builds no roads — it just tilts a vote toward your payout.
And the spend is **socially wasteful**: the money burned lobbying (and the matching money the *rival*
lobby burns pushing back) is value that vanishes. The game models this directly — your spend is gone
whether the bill passes or fails, and the rival lobby's pushback forces you to spend even more.

## Part 4 — Diminishing returns & the margin (where the dollar goes)

This is the game's interactive heart. Influence on any one official follows a **saturating curve**:
the first dollars move their vote a lot; later dollars move it barely at all. Each card's
**"next $D: +Δ%"** preview makes the shrinking marginal return visible in real time.

The skill — and the economics lesson — is **marginal allocation**: a rational spender puts the next
dollar where it buys the most outcome. In this game that's almost always a **cheap swing vote near
50%**. Spending on:
- a **sure thing** (already ~90% yes) is wasted — the marginal Δ is tiny;
- a **lost cause** (low lean, high price) is wasted — you can't reach the finish line cheaply;
- an **already-saturated** official is *worse* than wasted — it triggers scandal risk.

The banner's **PROJECTED pass-%** (computed exactly across the panel) is your feedback signal: watch
which official moves it most per dollar. That habit — equalize the marginal return across your options
— is the same logic that governs efficient spending of *any* scarce budget.

## Part 5 — Pushback, scandal, and limits

- **Competing interests.** Most real fights have lobbies on **both sides**. The rival lobby here
  steadily erodes your gains (and concentrates on the officials you're winning), so influence is not a
  one-time purchase — it decays, and you must out-spend or out-time the opposition.
- **Overreach has costs.** Pile too much onto one official and **scandal risk** builds; max it out and
  the deal blows up, costing you budget and the bill. A nod to real accountability pressures —
  disclosure rules, watchdogs, the press — that cap how brazen influence-buying can get.

## Part 6 — So is lobbying just bad?

No — and the honest version matters. Lobbying is **constitutionally protected petitioning**, and at its
best it's how affected groups bring real information and expertise to legislators who can't be experts
in everything. The *problem* public choice highlights isn't speech — it's the **structural asymmetry**:
organized, concentrated interests are systematically easier to mobilize than the diffuse public. The
game caricatures the cash to make that asymmetry vivid. The grown-up takeaway is to recognize the
incentive structure so you can spot it — subsidies and carve-outs that quietly tax everyone a little to
pay someone a lot — and ask "who's the concentrated interest, and who's diffusely paying?"

## Association verdict

**Strong tie to the financial scale.** The financial scale is about money as a system of incentives and
allocation under scarcity. Lobbying is that system pointed at *politics*: a budget, a market of votes
with prices, diminishing returns, competing buyers, and a clear objective function. It teaches the same
marginal-thinking muscle as Market Trader, applied to public choice instead of a price chart — and it
