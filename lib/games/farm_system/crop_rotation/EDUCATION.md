# Crop Rotation — Education (E)

**Topic:** crop rotation & soil health — why rotating crop families keeps a farm
productive, and why monoculture depletes soil and breeds pests and disease.

## The core idea
Different crops take different things from the soil and give different things
back. **Crop rotation** means deliberately changing which *family* of crop grows
in a field from one season to the next, instead of growing the same thing every
year (**monoculture**). Rotation is one of the oldest and most effective tools in
agriculture — the classic Norfolk four-course rotation (18th-century England)
cycled wheat → turnips (roots) → barley → clover (a legume), and roughly doubled
yields without letting fields lie idle.

## 1. Nitrogen: the soil's fuel gauge
Plants need **nitrogen** to grow leaves and stems, and it's the nutrient that
runs out fastest. In the game, every field has a **nitrogen bar**.
- **Heavy feeders** like **corn** (a cereal/grass) pull large amounts of
  nitrogen out of the soil. Big harvest, big drain — and corn only yields big if
  the soil is already rich.
- **Legumes** — beans, peas, clover — do something special: in nodules on their
  roots they host *Rhizobium* bacteria that **fix nitrogen** from the air into a
  form plants can use. When a legume crop finishes, it leaves the soil **richer
  in nitrogen than it found it**. That's why the bar goes *up* after beans.
- The classic move the game rewards: **legume → heavy feeder.** Beans recharge
  the soil, then corn cashes in the rich soil for a huge harvest.

## 2. Why monoculture depletes soil
Growing the same crop in the same field season after season takes the **same
nutrients** out every time, in the same proportions, with nothing putting them
back. The soil's nitrogen (and other minerals) is mined down faster than it can
recover, so yields fall year over year — exactly the compounding **crash** you
see when you plant one family twice on a field. Historically this is what forced
farmers into leaving fields **fallow** (unplanted) to recover — costly idle land.
Rotation lets the soil recover *while still producing a crop*.

## 3. Why monoculture breeds pests & disease
Every crop has its own specialist pests, fungi, and diseases. When the same crop
returns to the same ground every season, those pests find their food **waiting
for them** — populations build up year after year with no break, and an
outbreak can wipe out the harvest. Rotation **breaks the cycle**: when this
year's pest shows up and finds a crop it can't eat, its population collapses.
- **Root crops like potatoes** are especially good at breaking pest and disease
  cycles (and the act of digging tubers disturbs the soil), which is why
  planting potatoes in the game **resets** a field's pest pressure.

## 4. Putting it together — the rotation logic
A good rotation balances **what each family takes and gives**:
1. **Legume** (beans) — *adds* nitrogen → recharge the soil.
2. **Heavy feeder** (corn) — *spends* that nitrogen on a big harvest.
3. **Root** (potatoes) — breaks pest/disease cycles, opens the soil.
4. **Brassica** (cabbage) — a moderate feeder to round out the cycle.

Rotate through families and the soil, pests, and yields stay healthy
indefinitely. Repeat one family and everything degrades. That trade-off — the
whole point of crop rotation — *is* the game's scoring system.

## Takeaways
- Plants both **take** nutrients from soil and (legumes) **add** them.
- **Monoculture** drains soil and lets crop-specific **pests/diseases** build up.
- **Rotating crop families** keeps soil fertile and breaks pest cycles —
  productive farming without exhausting the land.
- Legumes **fix nitrogen**; roots **break pest cycles**; heavy feeders **cash in
  rich soil**. Sequence them and the farm thrives.
