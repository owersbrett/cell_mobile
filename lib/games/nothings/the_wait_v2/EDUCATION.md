# The Wait v2 — Education (E)

## The lesson: the internal clock
Humans estimate short durations with an **internal pacemaker–accumulator**, not
a literal clock. The brain emits pulses at a roughly steady rate and counts
them; "elapsed time" is how many pulses have accumulated. With **no external
cue** — no ticking, no bar, no second hand — your only instrument is that
internal count. This game *is* that instrument, isolated.

## What the game teaches IN the mechanic
The education is not a quiz bolted on; it is the scoring and the per-flash
insight.

- **Attentive waiting feels SLOWER → people drift LATE.** When you actively
  count and attend to time, more pulses register, so subjective time outpaces
  real time and you over-wait. The flash names this when you tap late.
- **Unmonitored time runs FASTER → people jump EARLY.** When attention lapses,
  fewer pulses accumulate and intervals feel shorter, so you tap early. The
  flash names this when you tap early.
- **Calibration is rare and trainable-ish.** Matching real time almost exactly
  ("DEAD ON") is uncommon without a clock — but the **tightening band** turns
  calibration into a measurable, improvable skill: can you hold accuracy as the
  tolerance shrinks from ±0.80s to ±0.22s?
- **Consistency beats luck.** The precision-streak multiplier rewards a player
  whose estimates have **low variance** across the run, mirroring how real
  timing skill shows up as reliability, not a single good guess.

## Why the tightening arc is pedagogically better than v1
v1's flat random 1–10s waits let a single lucky tap dominate and never tested
whether you could *hold* calibration. v2's descending targets + shrinking band
+ compounding streak make the lesson legible: you watch your own internal-clock
error under rising precision demand, round by round, on the pip standing.

## Talking points (for a session / lesson)
- "Why did almost everyone tap LATE on the 5-second wait?" → attentive counting.
- "Why does the 1.5-second final wait feel easier to nail?" → shorter intervals
  accumulate fewer pulses, so variance in real-time terms is smaller.
- "What does a full row of tight pips tell you?" → a calibrated, low-variance
  internal clock — the actual skill the game measures.
