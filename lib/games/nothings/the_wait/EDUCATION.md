# EDUCATION.md — The Wait

> The educational component (the **E** in GAMES). The Wait teaches **interval timing** and **internal time
> perception** — how the brain estimates the passage of seconds with NO external cue — and it teaches it
> *inside the mechanic*: the black wait IS the experiment, and the white flash IS the result readout.

---

## The core idea: you have an internal clock

Humans (and most animals) can estimate durations of seconds to minutes without counting devices. This is
**interval timing**, and it is distinct from the millisecond timing of motor control and the circadian
(~24h) clock. The Wait isolates interval timing by stripping away every external cue — no ticking, no bar,
no flicker — so the only thing left is your internal sense of a second.

Each round is a clean psychophysics trial:
- **Stimulus:** a target duration `N` (1–10 seconds).
- **Production task:** you *produce* that interval by tapping when you feel it has elapsed. (This is the
  "time production" paradigm — the inverse of "time estimation," where you'd judge a given interval.)
- **Response:** your actual elapsed time, scored by how close it is to `N`.

---

## What the flash teaches you, round by round

The white flash names your error so each round is a lesson:

- **LATE (over-waited).** You produced *more* time than asked. Paying close attention to time makes it feel
  **slower** — "a watched pot never boils" — so attentive counters tend to drift late on bare intervals.
- **EARLY (under-waited).** You produced *less* time than asked. When attention drifts off time, it feels
  **faster** ("time flies"), so unmonitored intervals tend to run early.
- **DEAD ON.** Your internal pacemaker matched real time — rare without a clock, and the goal.
- **NO TAP.** The 10s window closed. Long intervals are genuinely hard to hold in working memory without
  an external anchor, which is *why* clocks were invented.

---

## Why error grows with the target (Weber's law)

The scoring normalizes error by the target (`|elapsed − N| / N`) on purpose. Timing obeys an approximate
**scalar property** (a form of Weber's law): the *variability* of your estimates grows in proportion to the
interval being timed. A 5-second wait is roughly 5× as "wobbly" as a 1-second wait. So judging short and
long waits by the same *fractional* error is the fair, biologically honest measure — and it's why holding a
steady counting method matters more as `N` grows.

---

## The science, briefly

- **Pacemaker–accumulator model.** A classic account: an internal pacemaker emits pulses; an accumulator
  counts them; more pulses = more felt time. Arousal speeds the pacemaker (time dilates under fear/stress);
  attention gates which pulses get counted (distraction makes time shrink).
- **Dopamine and the clock.** Dopamine modulates the pacemaker's rate — stimulants make time feel like it's
  passing faster (you under-produce), and conditions/medications that lower dopamine slow it.
- **Counting strategies help.** Sub-vocal counting ("one-one-thousand, two-one-thousand") or a steady
  mental beat turns an unstable internal estimate into a more reliable motor rhythm — which is exactly the
  skill The Wait rewards.

---

## Takeaways a player should leave with

1. You carry an internal clock, and it is **biased and noisy** — usually a little late when you concentrate,
   early when you don't.
2. **Arousal and attention bend felt time** — the same 5 seconds feels different when you're calm vs. tense.
3. **Longer intervals are proportionally harder** (the scalar property) — precision is easiest at 1–2s.
4. A consistent **counting method** is the practical fix, and it's the difference between a guessy run and a
   high-scoring one.
